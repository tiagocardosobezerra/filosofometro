What it is

Empiricism holds that the mind starts as a blank slate and that everything we know comes from experience. Its central question is how far experience can take us. If we only know what we perceive, what can we say about things we never observe directly, such as causation, substance or the self? Taken seriously, empiricism sets clear limits on what we can claim with confidence.

It developed mainly in the British Isles in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, with John Locke as a key reference. The Irish bishop George Berkeley radicalized the idea: to be is to be perceived, and only minds and their perceptions exist. The Scot David Hume showed that we never see causation, only repeated sequences, and that our belief in it comes from habit. Their ideas influenced Kant, the philosophy of science and the experimental method.

Core ideas

  • All knowledge begins with the experience of the senses
  • There are no innate ideas: the mind starts as a blank slate
  • Belief in causation comes from habit, not pure reason
