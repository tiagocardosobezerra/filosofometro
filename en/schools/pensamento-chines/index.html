Confucianism and Taoism

The two great traditions of classical Chinese philosophy: one focused on social order and virtue, the other on harmony with the Tao.

What it is

Classical Chinese thought asks how to live in harmony with oneself, with others and with the world. Confucianism answers with cultivated virtues: respect for parents, sincerity, ritual and rulers who lead by example. Taoism answers with the Tao, the natural way of things, which cannot be forced: acting without forcing (wu wei), simplifying life and learning from water, which overcomes the hard by being soft.

These traditions arose in China between the sixth and third centuries BC, in an age of war and political crisis. Confucius taught that a good society begins with good personal conduct. Lao Tzu, to whom the Tao Te Ching is attributed, urged distrust of power and artificial rules. Yang Zhu, more radical, defended preserving one's own life rather than sacrificing it for the state. Together these views shaped East Asian culture for more than two thousand years.

Core ideas

  • A good society grows from virtue cultivated in each person
  • Acting without forcing, following the Tao, leads to harmony
  • Ritual and respectful relationships sustain life in common
